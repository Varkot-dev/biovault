# How to Autopsy a Trading Strategy

*Research notes prepared for evaluating the `tdncho-a11y/kjkjkj` strategy repo.
The repo itself is private and not yet accessible to Claude (see "Unblocking
access" at the end), so this document does the next best thing: it builds the

---

## The one-sentence version

A backtest is not evidence that a strategy works; it is evidence that the
strategy fit the past — and there are at least four standard ways a strategy
that is actually worthless (or actually a money-loser) produces a beautiful
backtest. Until each one is ruled out, a good-looking equity curve means
nothing.

All numbers below come from `pathology_results.json`, generated by
`backtest_pathology_demo.py` on synthetic data where we *know* the ground
truth. That's the point: when the data is a pure random walk by construction,
any "profit" the backtest finds is, provably, an illusion.

## Part 1 — The four ways a backtest lies

### 1. Selection bias / multiple testing ("we tried lots of things and kept the winner")

**Experiment 1:** one fixed, honestly-implemented strategy (20/100 moving-average
crossover, long/short) applied to **200 different assets that are pure random
walks** — zero predictability exists, by construction — over a typical retail
backtest window of 3 years.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Mean Sharpe across the 200 assets | −0.02 (as it should be: nothing to find) |
| Best of the 200 | **Sharpe 1.45** |
| Assets with Sharpe > 0.5 | 18.5% |
| Assets with Sharpe > 1.0 | 3.5% |

**Lesson:** if you (or your friends) tried the strategy on a bunch of coins /
tickers / timeframes and are showing the best result, a Sharpe of ~1.5 is
exactly what luck alone produces. The number of things you *tried* is part of
the evidence, and almost nobody reports it. This is the "multiple testing"
problem; Bailey & López de Prado's *Deflated Sharpe Ratio* paper exists
precisely to correct for it.

### 2. Overfitting / parameter mining ("we tuned the parameters until it worked")

**Experiment 2:** for each of 200 random-walk series, grid-search 26
(fast, slow) crossover parameter pairs on the first 5 years, keep the best,
then run those exact parameters on the next 5 years.

| Metric | In-sample (mined) | Out-of-sample (honest) |
|---|---|---|
| Median Sharpe | **0.52** | **−0.01** |
| Share above 1.0 | 9.5% | 2.5% |
| Share negative | — | 50.0% |

Best single example found: a 50/100 crossover with **in-sample Sharpe 1.63**
that scored **−0.64 out-of-sample**. The equity curve looks like a textbook
winner for five years, then bleeds. The data was noise the whole time.

**Lesson:** optimized parameters transfer *zero* skill when there is no real
edge — out-of-sample performance recenters on the truth (here: nothing).
Any strategy whose parameters were tuned on the same data used to grade it is
reporting luck, and more parameters/indicators = more ways to memorize noise.

### 3. Lookahead bias (the one-line bug that prints money)

**Experiment 3:** same code, same 200 pure-noise series, one changed line.
The correct version decides today's position from *yesterday's* signal
(`pos = roll(signal, 1)`). The buggy version uses the moving average computed
*including today's close* to trade today (`pos = signal`).

| Version | Median Sharpe on pure noise |
|---|---|
| Correct (signal lagged one day) | −0.06 |
| Lookahead bug | **2.26** — and 100% of the 200 series exceed Sharpe 1.0 |

**Lesson:** this is the single most common bug in amateur (and professional)
backtests, and it's the first thing to grep for in `kjkjkj`. Variants:
indicators computed on the full dataset before the loop; trading at the same
bar's close (or worse, open) using that bar's close; normalizing/scaling data
using the whole series' mean and std; labels built with future windows;
`shift()` in the wrong direction or missing entirely; intraday data with
end-of-day timestamps. If a backtest's Sharpe is > 2, lookahead is the prior.

### 4. Transaction costs, slippage, and turnover (real edges die here)

**Experiment 4:** this time the data has a *genuine* edge — AR(1) returns with
autocorrelation φ = 0.08, i.e. real daily momentum — and the strategy
(hold sign of yesterday's return) really captures it.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Median **gross** Sharpe | **0.99** — profitable on 100% of runs |
| Annual turnover | ~239× |
| Median **net** Sharpe at 10 bps/side | **−0.49** — profitable on only 9% of runs |

**Lesson:** a strategy with a real, statistically strong edge became a
money-loser under a *conservative* retail cost assumption (10 bps per side —
crypto taker fees are typically 5–10+ bps before slippage; spread and impact
add more). Costs scale with turnover, so fast strategies need enormous gross
edges. If the `kjkjkj` backtest is gross of fees — or assumes it fills at the
mid, at the close it just observed, or with unlimited liquidity — the results
are fiction until re-run with costs.

### The ones you can't demo offline (but must still check)

- **Survivorship bias:** backtesting only on assets that still exist today
  (today's top-100 coins, current S&P members) bakes in "it didn't die" —
  a massive hidden filter, easily worth several percent a year.
- **Data quality:** bad ticks, gaps, zero-volume bars that "fill" anyway,
  exchange outages, and (for crypto) whether the quoted prices were tradable
  at size on that venue at that time.
- **Regime dependence:** anything long-biased backtested on 2020–2021 or
  2023–2024 crypto "worked". So did buying and holding. Compare against that
  benchmark, and test 2022.
- **Shorting/funding reality:** perp funding rates, borrow costs, margin
  calls, and whether the venue even allowed the trade at that time.

## Part 2 — The audit checklist for `kjkjkj`

Run top to bottom once the code is readable. Every "no/unknown" is a red flag.

**Data**
1. Where does price data come from, and is it point-in-time (no revisions)?
2. Does the asset universe include things that later died or delisted?
3. Bar timestamps: does a "day t" bar close *before* the trade that uses it?

**Signal & execution**
4. Is every indicator computed only from data available at decision time?
   (Grep for: full-series `fit`/`mean`/`std`/`min`/`max`, missing `shift(1)`,
   `center=True` rolling windows, merging on future dates.)
5. What price does a trade fill at — same bar close (suspicious), next bar
   open (acceptable), or mid with slippage model (good)?
6. Are fees, spread, slippage, and funding modeled? At what rates? What is the
   annual turnover, and what is performance at 2× the assumed costs?
7. Position sizing: is leverage constant, or does it secretly scale with
   hindsight (e.g., volatility computed over the full sample)?

**Statistics**
8. How many strategies/parameter sets/assets/timeframes were tried before
   this one? (Be honest — every Discord-screenshot variant counts.)
9. Is there a true held-out period never touched during development?
10. Sample size: how many *independent* trades? Sharpe's standard error is
    roughly √((1+SR²/2)/T years); a Sharpe of 1.0 over 3 years has a 95% CI
    of about ±1.2 — i.e., statistically indistinguishable from 0.
11. Does performance survive small parameter perturbations (a 21/95 crossover
    should behave like 20/100 — if not, it's a mined artifact)?
12. Does it beat buy-and-hold of the same asset, risk-adjusted, net of costs?

**Code bugs to grep for specifically**
13. `dropna()` after signal creation (can silently align future rows).
14. Backfilled or forward-filled prices used in returns.
15. Reindexing/merging that aligns a signal with the return it predicted from.
16. Pandas `pct_change()`/`diff()` sign or off-by-one direction errors.
17. Equity curve computed on cumulative *sum* of leveraged returns without
    compounding/liquidation logic (hides ruin).

## Part 3 — How to validate properly (what "good" looks like)

1. **Freeze the rules, then walk forward.** Optimize on window 1, trade
   window 2 untouched; roll forward. Only the stitched out-of-sample record
   counts.
2. **Deflate the Sharpe.** Correct for the number of trials (Bailey &
   López de Prado, *The Deflated Sharpe Ratio*, 2014). Rule of thumb from
   Experiment 2: mining ~26 variants manufactures ~0.5 Sharpe from nothing.
3. **Cost stress test.** Report net performance at 1×, 2×, 3× assumed costs.
   A real strategy degrades; a turnover artifact dies instantly.
4. **Permutation test.** Shuffle (or block-shuffle) the returns, re-run the
   full *pipeline including optimization*, repeat 1000×. If the real data's
   result isn't in the top few percent of the shuffled distribution, the
   pipeline manufactures profit from noise (this is exactly Experiment 2's
   finding, White's "Reality Check" idea).
5. **Paper-trade forward.** The only unfakeable out-of-sample data is the
   future. Three to six months of live paper fills, compared against the
   backtest's prediction for the same period, settles most arguments.

**Reading list:** Bailey & López de Prado, "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio" (2014);
Harvey, Liu & Zhu, "…and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns" (2016);
López de Prado, *Advances in Financial Machine Learning* (2018), chapters on
backtesting and cross-validation leakage; Aronson, *Evidence-Based Technical
Analysis* (2006).
